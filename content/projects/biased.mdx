## The man with the notebook

There is a wholesale jaggery store in Sangli, Maharashtra. The owner has been running it for over a decade. He knows his regulars by name. He knows which months are slow and which are busy. He has a sense -- built from years of experience -- of when to stock up and when to hold back.

What he does not have is data he can actually use.

His records live in a notebook. Columns of dates, item weights, amounts in rupees. Every day, another row. Over years, thousands of rows. The information is all there. But ask him "why did March 2013 underperform?", and the notebook has no answer. Ask him "based on the last two years, should I place a large order before the festival season?" and the notebook just sits there.

This is not a story about one store. According to data from Dun & Bradstreet, for every 100 companies in India, more than 95 are micro enterprises. Four are small to medium businesses. Less than one is a large company. The commercial analytics firm called this the "Missing Middle" -- a structural gap where the vast majority of businesses exist without access to the analytical tools that large enterprises take for granted.

That jaggery store owner was the reason I started building B.I.A.S.E.D. during my undergraduate years. It is also the reason I rebuilt it, from scratch, four years later.

---

## The tools gap is wider than the revenue gap

When a company like Walmart or Tata wants to understand its business data, it has options. Salesforce Einstein starts at roughly $50 per user per month and integrates AI-driven forecasting directly into its CRM. Microsoft Copilot for Business costs around $30 per user per month and brings conversational AI into the Microsoft 365 ecosystem. IBM Watson requires enterprise pricing -- which is a polite way of saying "call us, and it will be expensive." Oracle Analytics Cloud, SAP Analytics Cloud, Tableau: the list goes on. Each of these is powerful. Each requires a budget, an implementation team, and ongoing subscription costs that simply do not exist in the economics of a small business.

The free alternatives do exist, but they are not really free in the way that matters for a non-technical business owner. Google Looker Studio is free but requires knowing how to connect data sources and build dashboards. Power BI Desktop is free to download but meaningful use requires Azure integration and technical knowledge. Open-source tools like Metabase or Apache Superset are genuinely powerful but assume someone on your team can install, configure, and maintain them.

The result is a predictable split. Large businesses get AI-driven forecasting, anomaly detection, and natural language queries over their data. Small businesses get Excel, if they are lucky, and intuition otherwise.

This is not a new observation. A 2017 joint study by ASSOCHAM (the Associated Chambers of Commerce and Industry of India) and PwC noted that smartphone penetration was creating a unique opportunity to reach small businesses that had never been reachable by traditional software. At the time, India had 468 million smartphone users. By 2022, that number was expected to cross 850 million. The hardware was there. The question was whether the software would follow.

For the most part, it did not.

## The first attempt (2017 to 2021)

During my undergraduate years at Walchand College of Engineering, I worked on the first version of what would become B.I.A.S.E.D. The idea was simple: build a smartphone application that any small business owner could use to store records, visualize trends, and get predictions about the future.

The technology we chose for prediction was LSTM, which stands for Long Short-Term Memory. It is a type of neural network particularly suited to time-series data -- data where the order of events matters, like daily sales figures. Traditional prediction methods like moving averages or exponential smoothing work reasonably well on stable, regular data. LSTM was a step up: it could learn longer patterns, handle irregular spikes, and improve as more data was fed into it.

We tested it on the jaggery store I mentioned. Real data: daily sales entries from March 2012 to April 2013. Sixty-one entries divided into forty for training and twenty-one for testing. The model started with modest accuracy. As it received more data, accuracy improved. The pattern held: the system got better the more it was used, which is exactly the right behavior for a tool meant to grow with a business.

The application had other features too. Multiple users could share a single workspace, managed through email-based login and verification. Administrators could add team members. Tags could be customized to any business type -- a grocery store would set up different categories than a beauty parlour or a jaggery wholesaler. Records could be viewed as graphs and pie charts. Predictions appeared as a forecast line.

We deployed it with a few local vendors. It ran. Real businesses used it. That part still matters to me: it was not a demo. It was not a proof of concept sitting in a GitHub repository. It ran on actual hardware, with actual data, for actual businesses.

But it had real limits. It was Android-only. It required server infrastructure that the business owner could not manage themselves. And most importantly, it could only do what we had explicitly programmed it to do. It could predict future sales based on past trends. It could not explain why sales had dropped. It could not answer a question it had not been designed to answer. It was a very good calculator for a specific set of problems, but it was not intelligent in any meaningful sense.

## What changed between 2021 and 2025

The honest answer is: everything.

In November 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT. By early 2023, it had over 100 million users -- the fastest consumer software adoption in history at that point. What ChatGPT demonstrated was not just that AI could generate text. It demonstrated that AI could understand language well enough to be genuinely useful in conversation. You could ask it a question in plain English and receive a thoughtful, structured answer. You did not need to know what model to use, what parameters to set, or how to write a query.

The models kept improving. Claude, Gemini, Llama, Mistral -- a generation of language models arrived that were not only capable but increasingly accessible. Some ran in the cloud. Some, like Llama and Mistral through a tool called Ollama, could run entirely on a personal laptop with no internet connection required.

The key technical development that made B.I.A.S.E.D. possible in its current form is something called Retrieval-Augmented Generation, or RAG. The name is technical but the idea is not.

A large language model trained on the internet knows a lot about the world. But it knows nothing about your business. It has never seen your sales records, your invoices, your expense reports. If you ask it "how did my business do last quarter?", it cannot answer, because it does not have your data.

RAG solves this by giving the model a way to look things up before it answers. Think of it this way: imagine you hire a very smart consultant. They are well-read, they understand business, they can reason about complex situations. But they just arrived today and know nothing about your specific company. Before they answer any question, they go to your filing cabinet, find the relevant documents, read them, and then form their answer based on what they found. That is RAG. The filing cabinet is your business data. The consultant is the language model.

The other key development is what is called an agentic workflow. Traditional question-and-answer AI goes in one step: you ask, it answers. An agentic workflow is more like how a good analyst actually works. When you ask a complex question, the system breaks it into sub-questions, investigates each one, checks whether the findings are consistent, and then synthesizes an answer. It can run multiple investigations in parallel. It can flag uncertainty. It can say "I found evidence for this, but I also found a conflicting data point you should look at."

These technologies existed in research labs in 2021. By 2024, they were available as well-maintained open-source libraries that anyone with programming knowledge could use. That is when I decided to rebuild B.I.A.S.E.D.

## What B.I.A.S.E.D. is today

The name is the same. The acronym is the same. The problem it is trying to solve is the same. Almost everything else is different.

B.I.A.S.E.D. today is an AI investigation engine for business data. The key word is "investigation," not "retrieval." It does not just find relevant records. It reasons about them.

Here is what using it actually looks like.

You bring your business data: sales records, expense logs, invoices, anything in a structured or semi-structured format. The system reads and understands this data not as raw rows and columns, but as meaning. It stores a semantic representation of your data using a technology called pgvector -- a database extension that can find information based on what it means, not just what it literally says. This means if you ask about "March performance" and your records say "third month," it will still find them.

You ask a question. In plain English. Something like: "My sales in the second week of April were unusually low. What might have caused this, and does the pattern match anything from previous years?"

B.I.A.S.E.D. does not answer immediately. It investigates. It uses a framework called LangGraph to run a multi-step process: it breaks the question into components, searches your data for relevant evidence, checks whether patterns from previous years match, identifies any conflicting information, and then assembles an answer. The answer comes with citations -- specific references to the records it used -- and a confidence score. If the evidence is thin or ambiguous, it tells you that too.

You can also explore scenarios. "What would my cash position look like in three months if sales stay flat but costs increase by 10%?" The system reasons through this against your actual historical data, not a generic financial model.

A few things worth being explicit about.

It runs entirely on your machine if you want it to. The Ollama integration means no data has to leave your computer. You are not sending your business records to a cloud server somewhere. For small business owners who are rightly concerned about data privacy, this matters.

It is free. The entire codebase is released under the Apache-2.0 license, which means you can use it, modify it, and build on it without paying anyone anything. The source code is at [github.com/theSelfSa/biased](https://github.com/theSelfSa/biased).

Multiple people can use it simultaneously. The same multi-user workspace design from the original version is preserved: one workspace, multiple team members, role-based access so administrators control who can see what.

It works on mobile as well as desktop, built as a Progressive Web App (PWA). Same device-agnostic philosophy as the original Android application, but now available on any platform without an app store.

## How a question becomes an answer

For readers who want to understand the mechanics without diving into code, here is the flow.

When you type a question, the system first converts it into a mathematical representation of its meaning -- a vector embedding. This is the same kind of representation used to store your business data. By converting both your question and your data into the same format, the system can find data that is semantically related to your question, not just data that contains the exact same words.

It then passes this context -- your question plus the most relevant portions of your data -- to a language model. If you have chosen to use Ollama, this model runs locally on your machine. If you have configured a cloud provider like OpenAI or Anthropic, it goes there. Either way, the model receives the evidence and reasons about it.

The LangGraph orchestration layer adds the multi-step investigation capability. Rather than a single pass, the system can run multiple queries, compare findings, check for inconsistencies, and build up a richer answer before responding. This is the agentic workflow: the system acts more like an analyst building a case than a search engine returning a list.

The MCP (Model Context Protocol) server layer means the system can also connect to external tools and data sources -- your accounting software, your inventory system, external market data -- and incorporate that into its reasoning.

The final answer includes the reasoning chain, the specific data points used as evidence, and a confidence assessment. This is intentional: a business owner making a real decision deserves to know not just what the AI thinks, but why, and how sure it is.

## The honest comparison

Here is a straightforward look at what B.I.A.S.E.D. offers compared to established paid tools:

| Tool | Cost | Conversational AI | Runs locally | Open source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Salesforce Einstein | $50+/user/month | Yes | No | No |
| Microsoft Copilot | $30/user/month | Yes | No | No |
| IBM Watson | Enterprise pricing | Yes | No | No |
| Zoho Analytics | $24/month | Limited | No | No |
| Google Looker Studio | Free (with limits) | No | No | No |
| Power BI Desktop | Free (basic) | No | No | No |
| **B.I.A.S.E.D.** | **Free** | **Yes** | **Yes (Ollama)** | **Yes (Apache-2.0)** |

What B.I.A.S.E.D. does not offer: a dedicated support team, a service level agreement, a polished enterprise UI, or guaranteed uptime from a managed infrastructure. These things matter for large organizations. For a small business owner who cannot afford $50 per user per month, they are not the relevant trade-off.

The relevant question is: can a beauty parlour owner, a grocery store owner, or a small logistics operator get useful, honest answers about their business data without spending money they do not have? That is the problem B.I.A.S.E.D. is built to solve.

## Who this is for

The original version of this project was tested on a jaggery wholesale store. That specificity matters. It was not tested on a hypothetical business with clean, complete, well-formatted data. It was tested on the actual records of an actual business: daily entries, some missing, some with unusual spikes, none of it formatted for a machine.

B.I.A.S.E.D. today is built for the same kind of businesses. Small retail: grocery stores, wholesale traders, boutiques, electronics shops. Service businesses: beauty parlours, repair workshops, tutoring centers, travel agencies. Any business where the owner or manager has data -- in some form, however messy -- and questions they cannot currently answer.

It is not built for Fortune 500 companies. Those companies have dedicated data teams, expensive tools, and the infrastructure to support them. B.I.A.S.E.D. is not trying to compete with Tableau or Power BI. It is trying to give businesses that cannot access those tools something genuinely useful.

The technical requirement to run it is: a computer and someone who can follow a setup guide. With Docker Compose, the entire system can be started with a single command. With Ollama, no API keys or cloud accounts are needed. The barrier is not zero, but it is as low as we know how to make it.

## Why this still matters

In 2017, when the first version of this idea was written up, the argument was about smartphone penetration opening a new channel to reach small businesses with technology they had never had access to. That argument was right, but the technology of the time could only go so far. LSTM could predict a trend line. It could not answer a question.

The argument today is different. The technology has arrived. Large language models that can understand plain-language questions and reason about specific data are not theoretical. They run on laptops. They are free to use. The infrastructure to build with them is open source and well-documented.

What has not arrived is the application layer that makes this accessible to the businesses that need it most. Salesforce and Microsoft have built excellent tools for organizations with the budget and technical infrastructure to use them. The Missing Middle is still missing.

B.I.A.S.E.D. is one attempt to close that gap. It is open source because the kind of democratization this problem requires cannot happen through closed software. It is free because the businesses that need it most are the ones who cannot pay. It runs locally because data privacy is not a luxury.

The jaggery store owner with the notebook should be able to ask his records a question and get an answer. That is what this project is for.

## For the technically curious

If you want to understand what is happening under the hood:

- **RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation):** The seminal paper by Patrick Lewis et al. at Facebook AI Research (2020) introduced this approach. [Read the paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401).
- **LSTM Networks:** The original architecture was proposed by Sepp Hochreiter and Jurgen Schmidhuber in 1997. [The paper](https://www.bioinf.jku.at/publications/older/2604.pdf) is still worth reading.
- **LangGraph:** The framework used for multi-agent orchestration in B.I.A.S.E.D. [Documentation](https://langchain-ai.github.io/langgraph/).
- **pgvector:** The PostgreSQL extension that enables semantic search over business data. [GitHub](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector).
- **Ollama:** The tool that makes running large language models locally practical. [ollama.com](https://ollama.com).
- **The "Missing Middle":** Dun & Bradstreet's analysis of India's SMB gap has been referenced in multiple economic policy discussions. The original framing appears in their India Business Register reports.
- **Apache-2.0 License:** The license under which B.I.A.S.E.D. is released. [What it means in plain English](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0).
