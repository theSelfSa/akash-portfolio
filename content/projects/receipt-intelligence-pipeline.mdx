## The problem with "just use AI"

Take a receipt from your last grocery run. Crumpled, photographed at an angle under fluorescent lights. The store's name is abbreviated. One item says `CHKN BRST LB` and costs `$6.79`. Another says `2% MILK GAL` but the price is cut off at the edge of the photo.

Now imagine doing that for 13 million receipts every single day , which is roughly what Fetch Rewards, the rewards app, processes. At that scale, you cannot have a person check every extraction. But you also cannot blindly trust AI to get everything right. A system that confidently produces wrong answers is worse than one that admits uncertainty.

That is the gap this project is designed to fill: not just extracting data from receipts, but knowing *when to trust the extraction* and *what to do when you don't*.

---

## Why receipt parsing is harder than it looks

Most people's intuition is: "just take a photo, run it through AI, get the data." That works fine for a demo. It breaks in production for a few specific reasons.

**Problem 1: OCR is just reading, not understanding.**

OCR , Optical Character Recognition , is the technology that turns an image of text into actual text your computer can work with. Think of it like a very fast typist who copies what they see, letter by letter. AWS Textract and Tesseract (the two OCR tools used here) are good at this. But they produce raw text, not structure.

The raw text for that grocery receipt might look like:

```
WHOLE FDS MKT
CHKN BRST LB    6.79
ORG MILK 2% GAL  5.49
SUB  12.28
TAX   0.98
TOTAL  13.26
```

That is not a structured record. It's a block of text. Turning it into `{merchant: "Whole Foods Market", items: [{name: "Chicken Breast", price: 6.79}, ...]}` requires understanding , not just reading.

![Raw OCR text on the left versus structured JSON with confidence scores on the right](/assets/projects/receipt-transform.png)

*What OCR gives you (left) versus what you actually need (right) , with per-field confidence scores showing how certain the model is about each extraction.*

**Problem 2: Every receipt is different.**

Gas stations, pharmacies, restaurants, grocery stores, food trucks , they all format receipts differently. There is no standard. A quantity might be `2x`, `x2`, `2 EA`, or just `2`. A discount might be labeled `SAVE`, `DISC`, `OFF`, or have no label at all. A system that works perfectly on Walmart receipts might fail completely on a farmers market handwritten receipt.

**Problem 3: AI can be confidently wrong.**

Large language models (the AI behind ChatGPT and similar tools) are excellent at interpreting ambiguous text. But they can also hallucinate , produce a plausible-sounding answer that is factually wrong. If a receipt's total is cut off and the model guesses `$24.50` when the actual total was `$42.50`, a financial app silently gets the wrong number. With no confidence tracking, you would never know.

## What I built and why

This project came out of a question I had after my internship at JAEA (Japan's national nuclear research lab), where I built a machine learning pipeline for detecting bubbles inside nuclear reactor tubes. The problem there was similar in structure: I had raw sensor data that needed to become structured information, limited examples to learn from, and consequences for getting it wrong.

The receipt problem is a civilian version of the same challenge. I wanted to build something that:

1. Extracts structured data from messy real-world documents
2. Knows when it's uncertain and routes those cases to a human
3. Learns from human corrections over time so the system gets better automatically
4. Is honest about its limitations in a benchmark

The result is a full pipeline , from image upload to structured data to quality review to continuous improvement , built as a real service, not a demo script.

## How it works, step by step

Think of the pipeline as an assembly line with a quality inspector at the end. Here is what happens to every receipt that enters the system.

### Step 1: The receipt arrives

A user uploads a photo or PDF of a receipt through the API. The system immediately responds with a receipt ID and a status of "processing." This is important: the system does not make you wait while it processes. It queues the work and sends you a ticket.

This pattern , accepting work immediately and processing in the background , is called **asynchronous processing**. It is how almost every modern web service works. When you post a photo on Instagram, it does not make your phone wait while it resizes and compresses the image for every screen size. It queues that work, confirms your upload, and processes in the background.

Here, the background processing is handled by **Celery** (a task queue) and **Redis** (an in-memory database that acts as the message broker between the API and the workers). Think of Redis as a bulletin board where the API posts jobs, and workers come and pick them up.

### Step 2: Reading the image (OCR)

The worker picks up the task and first tries to extract the raw text from the image. It uses **AWS Textract** as its primary tool , a cloud service from Amazon that is particularly good at documents and tables. If Textract is not available (for example, in local development without AWS credentials), it falls back to **Tesseract**, a free open-source OCR engine.

Textract does something useful beyond just reading text: it also returns a confidence score for each word. When it reads `CHKN BRST`, it might say it is 96% confident. When it reads a smudged total, it might say 71%. These word-level scores matter later.

### Step 3: Understanding the text (structured extraction)

Raw text goes in. Structured data needs to come out.

This is where a large language model (LLM) comes in. The system sends the raw OCR text to GPT-4o or a Groq-hosted model, along with a very specific instruction: "Return this as a structured receipt with these exact fields. Nothing else."

The "nothing else" part is enforced by a library called **Instructor**, which works with a tool called **Pydantic** to define exactly what shape the response must take. If the AI tries to return something that does not fit the schema , say, it puts text where a number should be , Instructor automatically retries with a correction prompt.

```python
class LineItem(BaseModel):
    name: str
    quantity: float
    unit_price: float
    total_price: float
    category: Optional[str]
    confidence: float  # the model's own assessment (0 to 1)
```

Notice the `confidence` field. The model is asked to assess its own certainty for each line item. This self-assessment, combined with the OCR confidence scores, feeds the next step.

### Step 4: The confidence decision

This is the most important step in the whole pipeline, and the one that separates it from a naive "call the AI" approach.

The system computes an overall confidence score for the extraction. It combines:
- The average OCR word confidence from Textract
- The model's self-assessed confidence for each field
- A set of sanity checks: does the subtotal plus tax equal the total? Is the date in a reasonable range? Does the merchant name look real?

Based on this score, three things can happen:

![Diagram showing receipts routed into three lanes based on confidence score](/assets/projects/confidence-routing.png)

*Confidence-based routing , the core quality mechanism. A human's time is spent only where the system is genuinely uncertain.*

| Confidence level | What happens |
|---|---|
| **High** (above 85%) | Auto-complete. The receipt is stored and marked done. |
| **Medium** (65 to 85%) | Specific fields are flagged for human review. |
| **Low** (below 65%) | The entire receipt goes into the review queue. |

This routing is the equivalent of a quality inspector on an assembly line who does not check every item , only the ones the machine flagged as uncertain. It scales. A human's time is spent where it matters.

### Step 5: Matching items to a catalog

Once line items are extracted, the system tries to match them to a canonical product catalog. "Canonical" just means the clean, standardized version , `"Whole Milk, 1 Gallon"` instead of `"MILK WHL 128oz"`.

Two approaches are used:

**Fuzzy matching** is the fast, first-pass approach. It looks for text similarity , how close is `MILK WHL GAL` to `Whole Milk, 1 Gallon`? If they are similar enough (above a threshold), it is a match.

**Semantic matching** is used when fuzzy matching is not confident enough. It converts the text into a numerical representation (called an **embedding**) that captures meaning, not just spelling. Two phrases that mean the same thing will have similar embeddings even if they share no words. These embeddings are stored in **pgvector**, a PostgreSQL extension that can find the most similar embedding to a query in milliseconds.

This is the same technology behind "search for anything and find relevant results even if you did not use the exact words."

### Step 6: Human review and the learning loop

Receipts that scored low confidence land in a review queue. A reviewer sees the original image side-by-side with the extracted data and can correct any field. These corrections are stored as ground truth.

![Cycle diagram showing four stages: human review, corrections stored, LLM error analysis, prompt improved , connected in a loop](/assets/projects/learning-loop.png)

*The learning loop , manual review stops being a cost center and becomes an optimization engine. Each stage feeds the next.*

Nightly, the system runs an error analysis job. It pulls all corrections from the past week, groups them by type (date format errors, price misattributions, quantity parsing failures), and asks the AI to identify patterns. The output is a suggested prompt fix , a change to the instructions given to the extraction model that would prevent the same class of mistake next time.

This is the learning loop. The system does not stay fixed at whatever quality it launched with. Human corrections become training signal, error analysis becomes prompt improvement, and the process repeats.

## The benchmark: honest numbers from a real test

The project includes a benchmark runner that tests extraction accuracy on the **CORD dataset** , a publicly available set of about 1,000 labeled receipts from Korean and international retailers, widely used in receipt understanding research. [CORD is available on HuggingFace](https://huggingface.co/datasets/naver-clova-ix/cord-v2).

I ran a 50-receipt sample using Groq (a fast, low-cost inference provider) under two conditions: a baseline prompt and a tuned prompt refined after one round of error analysis.

| Metric | Baseline | After tuning |
|---|---|---|
| Success rate | 86% | 90% |
| Total exact match | 30.2% | 44.4% |
| Line-item price exact | 22.6% | 34.4% |
| Line-item name fuzzy | 40.9% | 36.7% |
| Avg latency per receipt | 5.1s | 23.7s |

![Bar chart comparing baseline and tuned prompt across four metrics](/assets/projects/benchmark-chart.png)

*One round of error analysis moved total exact match from 30% to 44% , at the cost of a 4x latency increase. Line-item name matching regressed slightly due to Korean-language items in the dataset.*

A few things are worth noting honestly:

**The improvements are real but partial.** Numeric accuracy (totals, prices) improved substantially after tuning. This is the most important metric for financial use cases , getting a dollar amount wrong has direct consequences.

**Item name matching got slightly worse.** This is a known limitation: the CORD dataset contains Korean-language items that fuzzy matching handles poorly in its current configuration. Domain-specific dictionaries would help here.

**Accuracy comes at a latency cost.** The tuned configuration takes nearly five times longer per receipt. This is a genuine production tradeoff , better answers sometimes mean slower answers. In a system with asynchronous processing, latency is more manageable than in a synchronous one, but it still affects throughput.

**30 to 44% exact match is not a typo.** Exact match is a strict metric: if the extracted price is `$6.79` and the ground truth says `$6.80` (rounding difference), that counts as a miss. Fuzzy match scores, which allow for minor variation, are higher. Real production systems often use fuzzy match thresholds for evaluation.

These numbers are a starting point, not a ceiling. The benchmark infrastructure exists precisely so improvements can be measured rather than assumed.

## What this is not

It is worth being clear about scope.

This is a **research and demonstration project**, not a deployed production service. It is designed to show the architecture, the active learning loop, and the benchmark methodology , not to handle millions of receipts at commercial scale. At that scale, additional concerns arise: distributed worker fleets, multi-region redundancy, stricter SLAs, billing integrations, and much more.

It also does not yet handle handwritten receipts, non-Latin scripts beyond what Textract supports, or receipts with embedded images of products rather than text descriptions.

## Key concepts, if you want to go deeper

For those who want to understand the underlying ideas better:

- **OCR (Optical Character Recognition):** [AWS guide to OCR](https://aws.amazon.com/what-is/ocr/) , a good plain-English explanation
- **Large Language Models:** [What is a large language model?](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/ai/what-is-large-language-model/) , Cloudflare's accessible overview
- **Active Learning:** The idea of letting a model flag which examples it is most uncertain about, then having a human label only those. [A visual explanation from Google](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/developers-practitioners/active-learning-machine-learning)
- **Vector embeddings and semantic search:** [What are embeddings?](https://openai.com/blog/introducing-text-and-code-embeddings) , OpenAI's explanation of how meaning becomes numbers
- **pgvector:** [pgvector on GitHub](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector) , how PostgreSQL handles similarity search
- **Weak supervision:** Using noisy or imperfect labels (like LLM outputs) as training data when clean labels are expensive. [The Snorkel paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10160) introduced this idea formally
- **CORD dataset:** [CORD on HuggingFace](https://huggingface.co/datasets/naver-clova-ix/cord-v2) , the receipt benchmark used here
